# Selection-conditioned laws in lattice reduction

**Finding.** Vallée and Vera's inverse-branch calculation is the most directly transferable technique. It computes the input mass on which a particular reduction decision wins and the output passes a test. In a planar family below, this gives a closed-form selected-quotient probability. On a finite grid, the selected hit has probability $2/11$, while an incidence drawn uniformly from the eligible candidate incidences wins with probability $2/13$. Exact Gauss reduction verifies both counts. I found no published theorem giving the corresponding weighted prefix estimate for the width-506 restart.

## What a theorem would have to measure

Part 164 uses two measures. Under the experiment on inputs, $P$ is the history average, restricted to $G$, of the fraction of final marks for which the **designated** candidate passes the band and both gates. Under the experiment on incidences in its equation (4), a prefix $S$ has weight proportional to $\Pr(S)|I_S(f)|/|H(S)|$ for an observed group $f$. Its contribution to the numerator counts only winning incidences. Canonical witnesses eliminate an additional witness multiplicity. A compatible positive-height candidate in the band fixes the final mark, so the comparisons are already decided conditional on the prefix and incidence. Both the distribution of prefixes and the weights within a prefix matter. The sufficient target through Part 172 is $P+p_a\le 0.00019770868$, including the separate inherited residual. This is the direct geometric criterion discussed in Part 171. ## Which analyses follow the algorithm's selections?

**Planar Gauss reduction.** Vallée and Vera, *Lattice reduction in two dimensions: analyses under realistic probabilistic models* (2007), Propositions 2–3 and Theorem 1, specify the admissible fractional-linear maps for variants of Gauss reduction. For a planar input density $f$ on the indicated starting domain, the density on the terminal domain is $\sum_h |h'(z)|^2 f(h(z))$. Each term is the mass brought by a disjoint inverse image. Restricting the sum to branches with a specified digit history gives a *joint* mass. Dividing by the mass of that history gives its conditional law. This handles comparisons along an actual run. The elementary measure identity behind the theorem also works for atomic inputs, by counting the atoms in each preimage. Its Jacobian formula assumes an area density. Part 127 identified this paper already. The new calculation below evaluates both measures for one atomic input law and the selected probability for a continuous input on the same line. [Original paper, §4.1, Theorem 1](https://dmtcs.episciences.org/articles/3549/download) · [Part 127](wall1_adjacent_methods.md)

**Conditioning on survival.** Daudé, Flajolet and Vallée, *An Average-Case Analysis of the Gaussian Algorithm for Lattice Reduction* (1997), analyze the standard planar algorithm under their uniform continuous shape model and also study discretization. They obtain a finite mean iteration count, a geometrically decreasing iteration tail, and a conditionally invariant measure. Restricting the input to branches that execute another iteration before transporting the measure retains the bias among continuing runs. A survival law answers a different question from the joint mass of a specified terminal gate and its winning line. The planar branches and their distortion estimates are specific to the Gauss map. Vallée and Vera also give an explicit sum over length-$k$ inverse branches for the iteration tail in their valuation models. [Daudé–Flajolet–Vallée, CPC 6, 397–433](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0963548397003258) · [Vallée–Vera, §5, theorem attributed to Daudé–Flajolet–Vallée](https://dmtcs.episciences.org/articles/3549/download)

**Average LLL runtime and random initial bases.** Daudé and Vallée bound the expected number of iterations for $p$ independent uniform vectors in the unit ball in $\mathbb R^n$. For fixed reduction parameter and $p/n$ bounded away from one, their bound is $O(n\log n)$. It comes from averaging a deterministic execution bound over the initial Gram–Schmidt data. Akhavi, Marckert and Rouault, Theorem 1.4, instead analyze the probability that the *initial* basis satisfies the local reduction test. Their vectors are independent with rotation-invariant law satisfying radial concentration assumption (1.2). For fixed $s\in(0,1)$, that probability tends to one if the codimension diverges and to a number strictly between zero and one if the codimension is fixed. Their main formulation uses the Siegel ratio criterion. Neither calculation gives the law of the retained basis after comparisons. The survey *Probabilistic Analyses of Lattice Reduction Algorithms* distinguishes these results from its two-dimensional output laws. [Daudé–Vallée (1994)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(94)90071-X) · [Vallée–Vera, book chapter 3, Theorem 3](https://www.ionica.nl/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/the-LLL-Algorithms.pdf) · [Akhavi–Marckert–Rouault, Theorems 1.4 and 1.8](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0604331)

**Sandpiles.** Madritsch and Vallée's *Modelling the LLL algorithm by sandpiles*, Theorem 2, concerns a totally non-reduced starting basis and a $\rho$-regular execution. Regularity fixes the decrease factor across the run. Under this hypothesis they bound the normalized logarithm of the first output length, and Theorem 3 analyzes the iteration count with their Mod-Exp-Ajtai input. The hypothesis has no proved probability for the restart. Ding, Kim, Takagi, Wang and Yang's *A physical study of the LLL algorithm*, Theorems 1 and 5, states an average/worst-case root-Hermite-factor gap for the stochastic sandpile SSP in sufficiently large system size. Their Conjecture 6 concerns extending a gap to LLL-SP, itself a stochastic model. The random updates define the surrogate law. Identifying its terminal law with a selected lattice window would require a separate theorem. [Madritsch–Vallée, §§1.5 and 4](https://madritsch.perso.math.cnrs.fr/downloads/papers/latin-lncs.pdf) · [Ding et al., §§1 and 3](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.02158)

**Reduced-basis counts and measured profiles.** Kim and Venkatesh's Theorem 1.1 gives a mean count of Siegel-reduced bases for a Haar lattice, concentration of the count assuming the Riemann hypothesis, and a length law for normalized measure on the Siegel set. The count gives one vote per qualifying basis. It supplies no basin size for a deterministic reducer. Nguyen and Stehlé explicitly distinguish these two sampling rules in *LLL on the Average*, §§3.3–3.4. They measure actual LLL outputs for Goldstein–Mayer, knapsack-type and Ajtai-type input bases. Bai, Stehlé and Wen measure actual BKZ outputs on LLL-preprocessed Darmstadt knapsack-type bases with a prime of about $10n$ bits. Their Theorem 1 concerns shortest lengths for Haar lattices in the large-dimension limit. Using its exponential volume law for the next block in Algorithm 3 is a modelling assumption. These experiments include the algorithmic decisions in each observation. The papers give no finite-error comparison for Part 164's incidence-weighted law. [Kim–Venkatesh, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.00767) · [Nguyen–Stehlé, corrected paper](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/damien.stehle/downloads/average-corrected.pdf) · [Bai–Stehlé–Wen, §§2–4](https://eprint.iacr.org/2018/856.pdf) · [Part 107](wall1_lit_random_bases.md)

**A rigorous limit for a q-ary HKZ output.** Fix rank $n\ge2$, take a uniformly random hyperplane modulo a prime $q$, lift its inverse image to $\mathbb Z^n$, and scale to covolume one. This samples each index-$q$ sublattice once, since every such sublattice contains $q\mathbb Z^n$. Goldstein–Mayer's equidistribution theorem therefore gives weak convergence to Haar as $q$ tends to infinity. The first **global HKZ** output length is $\lambda_1$, a continuous function of the lattice. Hence its distribution converges to the Haar shortest-length law at continuity points. This is a deduction for a global, basis-independent observable and a specified varying-modulus ensemble. At finite $q$, Lindner–Peikert's §5.1 reports measured GS profiles of BKZ on random q-ary inputs and invokes GSA to predict lengths. Neither result assigns weights to signed histories of overlapping windows. [Goldstein–Mayer, *On the equidistribution of Hecke points*](https://doi.org/10.1515/form.2003.009) · [Lindner–Peikert, §5.1 and Fig. 2](https://eprint.iacr.org/2010/613.pdf) · [Earlier projection survey](wall1_lit_projections.md)

## A comparison with pivoting, records, matching and dependent counts

**Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting gives a high-dimensional precedent.** For an $n\times n$ matrix with independent standard Gaussian entries, Huang and Tikhomirov, Lemma 4.1, replay the first $r$ pivots on a prescribed $r$-row subset. It is the actual pivot subset precisely when every outside row belongs to a polytope $K$ cut out by those comparisons, almost surely. Given the prescribed rows, the probability of this event is $\sigma_n(K)^{n-r}$, with $\sigma_n$ Gaussian measure. Given also that these rows won, the remaining rows are independent Gaussians restricted to $K$. Proposition 4.2 proves $\Pr(\sigma_n(K_r(A))\le n^{-t})\le n^{-t(n-r)/2}$ for $r<n, t\ge2$. This controls a random comparison region under the **actual pivot history**. The product works because every outside comparison tests a separate independent input row after the proposed pivot rows are fixed. In Part 164 all the compatible competitors at a fixed positive-height incidence use the same mark. Independent input columns before reduction do not imply this product property for competitors. [Huang–Tikhomirov, §4, Lemma 4.1 and Proposition 4.2](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00440-024-01276-2)

| Method | The selection law it controls | Limitation for the weighted ratio |

Deleting the last arrival successively gives a bijection from the $m!$ equally likely orders to the $\prod_i i$ insertion-rank strings. Thus record indicators are independent with probabilities $1/i$. | Restart comparisons use scores that change after projection, and ties have positive mass. Exchangeability of input columns does not give uniformly ordered candidate scores. [Part 166](wall1_random_order.md) |

## Exact planar transfer

Take the basis $e=(1,0), z=(x,1/6)$ with $1/3\le x\le1/2$. Ask for the probability that the lattice projected perpendicular to the Gauss-selected shortest line has spacing **strictly less than $5/12$**. This is a short-quotient test in a single plane. The full restart also selects a line in the first quotient and tests a plane there. The planar example isolates one selection and permits exact input counts.

For a primitive line of length $s$, the projected spacing is $1/(6s)$, by extending its generator to a lattice basis and taking the area. Thus the test after forcing that line is $s>2/5$. Only the lines $v=z$ and $w=2z-e$ can win. Indeed $w$ has length at most $\sqrt{2}/3<1/2$ throughout the interval. In $ae+bz$, every $|b|\ge3$ gives vertical component at least $1/2$, while nonzero horizontal vectors have length at least one. For $b=1,2$ the displayed candidates minimize the absolute horizontal component. The $b=1$ nearest-integer tie at $x=1/2$ has length greater than $1/2$.

The input partition and the gates now follow from the two squared lengths.

$$
s_v^2=x^2+1/36,\qquad s_w^2=(1-2x)^2+1/9.
$$

The winning branch changes from $v$ to $w$ at $c=(4-\sqrt{3})/6$. The $v$-gate holds for $x>L=\sqrt{119}/30$, and the $w$-gate for $x<U=1/2-\sqrt{11}/30$. Since $L<c<U$, the inverse image of a selected hit is $(L,U)$. Either tie choice at $c$ gives a hit. With uniform Lebesgue probability on the interval the answer is

$$
6(U-L)=3-\frac{\sqrt{119}+\sqrt{11}}{5}.
$$

This is the inverse-branch calculation on the input support. The two pieces are the gated inputs on each *winning* branch. The two forced gate sets alone cover the entire input interval.

**Independent finite check.** Give equal mass to the eleven points $x=j/60, 20\le j\le30$. Scaling both coordinates by 60 gives an integer basis $(60,0),(j,10)$. Compare squared lengths with $24^2$ for the forced gate. Nearest-integer Gauss reduction on each of the eleven inputs and direct length comparison give the same winners. The integer expressions $j^2+100$ and $(2j-60)^2+400$ reproduce every entry below.

| Line | Indices at which it wins | Indices passing its forced gate | Indices satisfying both |
|---|---|---|---|
| $v$ | 20–22 | 22–30 | 22 |
| $w$ | 23–30 | 20–23 | 23 |

Define the incidence experiment by drawing an input and then choosing uniformly between the two branch candidates $v,w$, including a candidate when its forced gate passes. Every input supports at least one eligible incidence. Of the 22 input–candidate pairs, thirteen are eligible and two of those win. Grouping by candidate gives conditional winning fractions $1/9$ and $1/4$. Their incidence weights are $9/13$ and $4/13$, giving $2/13$. Each input instead contributes one designated winner to the algorithmic distribution, yielding $2/11$. Equivalently the incidence count per input, $13/11$, times its conditional winning fraction, $2/13$, gives the selected mass. The grid has no shortest-line tie. All probabilities here are exact for the stated planar laws.

**Remaining conjecture.** For plain LWE $(511,512,3329)$, CBD(3), the quoted inequality for $P+p_a$ remains open. What transferred was the ability to pull the joint gate and winner test back to inputs, with its correct incidence weights. In the example all possible winning lines reduce to two explicit branches, and their input masses follow from interval endpoints. For the restart, Part 164 already has the corresponding exact identity. None of the checked theorems evaluates the weighted mass of its comparison regions or controls the inherited residual at the required scale. ## Audit

**Verdict — MINOR CAVEATS.** The selected and incidence probabilities, the continuous branch boundaries, and the stated scope of transfer to Part 164 check out. One source attribution merits clarification.

**Independent planar check.** An exact scratch calculation started with the two integer basis vectors, repeatedly put the shorter vector first, and subtracted the nearest integer multiple of it from the second until the rounded quotient was zero. The squared lengths of the first output vector, in order from $j=20$ to $30$, were $500,541,584,596,544,500,464,436,416,404,400$. Only $j=22,23$ exceed $576$. At both of these inputs both forced lines pass, with exactly one shortest line. Across all eleven inputs the two forced lines pass nine and four times respectively. This gives $2/11$ on uniform inputs and $2/13$ after conditioning a uniform input–candidate pair on eligibility.

For the continuous check, $s_w^2-s_v^2=3x^2-4x+13/12$ has just the stated root $c$ in the interval. The winning squared length is the minimum of the increasing $s_v^2$ and decreasing $s_w^2$. It exceeds $4/25$ exactly when both exceed $4/25$, yielding the open interval $(L,U)$ including $c$. Its uniform mass is $6(U-L)$. Both planar examples use a preimage under the selection rule. Even the continuous example has support on a line and hence uses its one-dimensional input measure. Vallée–Vera's area-density formula itself requires an area density. [Vallée–Vera, Theorem 1](https://dmtcs.episciences.org/articles/3549/download)

**Attribution and source coverage.** Read “their Theorem 1” in the Bai–Stehlé–Wen paragraph as a reference to a result they invoke. Their conference presentation explicitly credits Chen, Corollary 3.1.4, and Södergren, Theorem 1, for the limiting exponential law of the volume at the first minimum of a Haar lattice. The following slide uses an exponential draw in Algorithm 3. The presentation confirms that distinction and the comparison with measured BKZ output. The ePrint PDF returned HTTP 403 during this audit, so the precise theorem numbering and the prime bit length in that version were not independently confirmed. [Bai–Stehlé–Wen, slides 8–11](https://asiacrypt.iacr.org/2018/files/SLIDES/MONDAY/514/1330-1445/Bai_slides.pdf) · [Södergren](https://arxiv.org/abs/1001.3623)

The cited statements preserve the distinctions between Daudé–Flajolet–Vallée's survival law, the Daudé–Vallée iteration bound, Akhavi–Marckert–Rouault's test on the input basis, and Nguyen–Stehlé's measured outputs. More precisely, Kim–Venkatesh Theorem 1.1(i) gives the leading asymptotic of the logarithm of the mean count. The Riemann hypothesis enters (ii), the relative fluctuation estimate. Part (iii), the length concentration under normalized measure on the Siegel set, is unconditional. The sandpile statements retain their model or regularity hypotheses. [Daudé–Flajolet–Vallée, publisher abstract](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0963548397003258) · [Akhavi–Marckert–Rouault, Theorem 1.4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/0604331) · [Kim–Venkatesh, Theorem 1.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1608.00767) · [Nguyen–Stehlé](https://perso.ens-lyon.fr/damien.stehle/downloads/average-corrected.pdf)

**Transfer.** A uniform prime-index sublattice gives the stated fixed-rank weak limit for the global first minimum by continuity. For Part 164, each compatible incidence fixes the mark. After canonicalizing witnesses, its mass is the prefix probability divided by the number of marks. The pivot comparison uses independence of outside rows conditional on the proposed pivot rows. The matching, coupling and Poisson results likewise require hypotheses on the law being sampled. None of these checked results estimates the sum of the winning incidence masses or the separate inherited residual. The sufficient bound on $P+p_a$ remains open.
