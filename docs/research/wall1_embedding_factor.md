# Terminal recovery as a function of embedding height

For a **fixed integral prefix**, the set of successful squared embedding heights is an open interval, possibly empty or unbounded. This holds even for a tilted prefix. Divisibility in the quotient is constant throughout the deformation. A separate obstruction, a tie with another vector of the same height, can persist at every scale even when divisibility is one.

## Fixed-prefix theorem

Use the integral lattice and mark $u=(x,1)$ of [the planted model](usvp_planted.md). Apply $T_\\tau=\\operatorname{diag}(1,\\ldots,1,\\tau)$ to the whole lattice, with $\\tau>0$, and put $s=\\tau^2$. Fix a primitive prefix $W$ of rank $k<d$ before varying the metric. Success means the projected mark and its negative are exactly the shortest vectors of the projected lattice, as in [Part 8](wall1_planted_flags.md).

The map $T_\\tau$ identifies the abstract quotients before and after deformation. If $[u]=0$ or $[u]$ is divisible by an integer greater than one, the success set is empty. In particular the prefix generated by $b_1,\\dots,b_{k-1},2b_k+u$ in [Part 9](wall1_planted_prob.md) has multiplicity two for every height. Its membership under a given covolume cutoff is a separate metric question.

Otherwise fix basis columns $B$ for $W$. For each $z$ outside the planted line in the quotient, form $M_z=[B,z]$. Let $A_z$ be the sum of squared maximal minors omitting the last row and $C_z$ the sum of those using it. The exact success set consists of all $s>0$ satisfying, simultaneously,
$$
(A_z-A_u)+s(C_z-C_u)>0.
$$
Indeed Cauchy–Binet gives $A_z+sC_z$ for the squared volume after scaling. Dividing by the common squared volume of the scaled prefix gives the squared projected length, by base times height. Multiples of the primitive planted class already satisfy the required comparison apart from the two generators. The inequalities therefore prove convexity, including when there are infinitely many classes.

For openness, near any $s_0>0$ restrict to $s_0/2<s<2s_0$. Squared norms are between one half and twice the norms squared at $s_0$, and taking the infimum over real prefix translates preserves both bounds. Only quotient classes whose length at $s_0$ is at most twice the marked length there can compete anywhere in this neighborhood. There are finitely many because a primitive lattice prefix extends to a basis, whose remaining projected columns form a lattice basis. Finite strict inequalities give local openness.

## Horizontal prefixes and a persistent tie

For horizontal $W$, write $Y$ for the horizontal quotient lattice and $p$ for the projected horizontal part of the mark. Every competing class has the form $(y+jp,j\\tau)$ with $0\\ne y\\in Y$. The squared-length difference is exactly
$$
\\|y+jp\\|^2-\\|p\\|^2+(j^2-1)s .
$$
Thus level zero imposes an upper bound $s<\\lambda_1(Y)^2-\\|p\\|^2$ when $Y$ has positive rank. Levels $|j|\\ge2$ impose lower bounds. Levels $|j|=1$ require $p$ to lie in the interior of the Voronoi cell of zero in $Y$, independently of height. This follows by comparing $p$ with every translate $p+y$ and using $Y=-Y$.

For an explicit Euclidean example take horizontal generators $(4,0),(2,4)$, mark $(0,2,\\tau)$, and prefix generated by $(4,0,0)$. This prefix is a shortest horizontal line since a nonzero second coordinate has magnitude at least four. The horizontal quotient is $4\\mathbb Z$ and $p=2$. The classes $(2,\\tau)$ and $(-2,\\tau)$ tie on distinct lines for every positive height. At height two the ambient mark is nevertheless uniquely shortest up to sign. Its squared length is eight. Level zero has nonzero squared lengths at least sixteen, and levels of absolute value at least two have vertical squared length at least sixteen. At level one the horizontal coordinates are $(4a+2b,2+4b)$. Squared horizontal length at most four requires $b=0$ or $-1$ and first coordinate zero, which forces $b=a=0$. Negation handles level minus one. This example concerns horizontal reduction in a general integral lattice.

## What is missing for a selected prefix

For each input and each possible output prefix the theorem gives a deterministic success interval. At a prescribed height the probability asked for is the sum over prefixes of the probabilities of the joint events that reduction selects that prefix and its interval contains the squared height. Both events depend on the same input. The interval theorem gives no bound on these joint probabilities, and a change of height may change the selected prefix. No block-size improvement over the existing certificates follows here.
